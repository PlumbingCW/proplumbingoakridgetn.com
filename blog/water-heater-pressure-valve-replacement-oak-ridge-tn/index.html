How to Replace a Water Heater Pressure Valve: An Oak Ridge, TN Homeowner Guide

The pressure valve on your water heater is a safety device, and a drip from it is a message. Here is what the valve does, how a replacement works and where a homeowner should stop and call.

By the Oak Ridge Pro Plumbing team · Published November 16, 2026 · Updated November 16, 2026

The pressure valve on a tank water heater is formally called the temperature and pressure relief valve, or TPR valve. It sits on the side or top of the tank with a pipe running down from it. Its only job is to open if the tank gets too hot or the pressure climbs too high. If it never opened, a failed thermostat could turn the tank into a hazard.

Because it is a safety device, treat it with respect. If yours drips, leaks steadily or discharges hot water, the answer is not to cap it or plug the pipe. Find out why. If it is an emergency leak, our water heater repair team is on the line 24/7.

Why a relief valve drips

A valve can drip for three different reasons, and only one of them means the valve is bad. The first is a worn seat, since the valve opens a little over time and debris or scale keeps it from closing fully. The second is high water pressure in the house, which pushes the valve open. The third is thermal expansion, where heated water takes up more space and has nowhere to go.

Drip causes and what to do
CauseClueFix
Worn or scaled valve seatSmall steady drip, normal pressureReplace the valve
High house pressureBanging pipes, several fixtures drippingTest pressure, replace the regulator
Thermal expansionDrips when the heater runs, stops afterAdd an expansion tank
Overheating tankHot, steaming dischargeShut off power or gas and call now

A new valve on a house with high pressure will drip too. That is why we test the pressure before we replace a valve, and why a plumber's visit can save you from buying the same part twice.

What a replacement involves

The work is simple in principle and easy to get wrong in practice. The plumber turns off the power or gas, closes the cold water supply, opens a hot tap and drains enough water to bring the level below the valve. Then the old valve comes off, the new one goes on with thread sealant and the discharge pipe is reconnected.

  1. Switch off the electric breaker, or set the gas control to off.
  2. Close the cold water shutoff above the heater.
  3. Open a hot tap in the house to release pressure and let air in.
  4. Drain a few gallons from the tank drain into a bucket or floor drain, to bring the level below the valve.
  5. Unscrew the old valve, and note the discharge pipe it feeds.
  6. Seal and thread in a new valve of the same rating, and reconnect the pipe.
  7. Refill the tank, check for leaks and restore power or gas.

The rules that matter more than the wrench

The discharge pipe must run downward to a safe place, with no valves, caps or threaded end. It must not be reduced in size. A pipe that points at the floor next to a child or a pet, or one that ends in a plug, defeats the valve. The replacement valve must also match the tank's pressure and temperature rating, which is printed on the tank label.

When to stop and call a plumber

  • The valve is discharging steaming or very hot water.
  • The tank is old or already leaking at the bottom.
  • The shutoff valve above the heater will not close.
  • You smell gas or see scorched wiring near the tank.
  • You cannot find a drain, a bucket path or the breaker with confidence.

Test the valve, then test the pressure

Most manufacturers suggest lifting the test lever briefly once a year to confirm that the valve opens and reseats. Do it with a bucket under the discharge pipe and stand clear. If the valve does not reseat and keeps dripping afterward, it needs replacing.

If you suspect high pressure, a gauge that screws onto a hose bib shows it in a minute. Pressure above the range printed on your fixtures and heater should be checked by a plumber. A failed regulator is a common reason for repeat drips, and replacing it is a different job from replacing the valve.

What this looks like in an older Oak Ridge house

Many Oak Ridge homes are decades old, and older tanks may sit in closets or carports with tight clearances. A valve in a closet might discharge into a pipe that was never routed properly. A corroded fitting beside the valve can snap when you try to unscrew it. If your tank is in a tight space, expect more trouble than the part cost suggests.

The relief valve must carry an ANSI Z21.22 rating that matches the tank, and the discharge pipe follows the manufacturer's instructions and the local code, based on the International Plumbing Code (IPC) or the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC).

Repair, replace or watch it

A new valve on an old tank buys you time only if the tank is sound. If the tank is near the end of its life, a water heater replacement may make more sense than a part.
